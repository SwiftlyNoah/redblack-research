\title{\bfseries Decoupled Equilibrium Selection:\\[2pt]
       \large Quantal-Response Values, CFR+ Policies,\\
       and Where Pruning Belongs in a Decomposed Solver}
\author{Noah Brauner}
\date{}

\begin{document}

\vspace{-3.2em}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A decomposed solver for a large imperfect-information game stores two
different products for every subgame: the values its parents bootstrap
from, and the policy its deployment plays. This paper reports a measured
finding about heads-up Red/Black, a two-player zero-sum hidden-information
card game with $2.448\times10^{33}$ perfect-recall information sets: the
properties those two products need are supplied by different equilibrium
selection principles, and nothing forces one method to produce both.
Value correctness under composition requires uniqueness and equivariance,
which an annealed quantal-response ladder supplies (colour-swap residual
$3.5\times10^{-9}$ per cell against a CFR+ baseline's $1.67$); playing
strength requires sharp support, which the same ladder's smoothed
policies give up (a belief-carrying best-response probe reads a lower
bound of $+0.076$ against the annealed profile versus $+0.031$ against
the sharp baseline it was meant to replace). We couple the two: every
subgame is re-solved with CFR+ against the quantal-response values held
byte-identically fixed, and the values are deployed unchanged while
only the policies are replaced. The coupled profile is the first in this program's
history that the probe cannot beat, and at $10{,}000$ paired games per
arm the probe loses to it: the reading is $-0.0248$ $[-0.0444,
-0.0052]$ against the incumbent's $+0.0342$ $[+0.0146, +0.0538]$,
a difference of $-0.0590$ at $p=3.0\times10^{-5}$, with the same
direction and significance on the $6{,}800$ fresh games that took no
part in selecting the candidate. A negative lower bound is vacuous as a
bound, so the claim it supports is comparative, and what it says about
the probe is that this instrument has bottomed out. A
contrast experiment locates where the surgery must not happen: baking a
real-game-motivated pruning of a dominated action into the solved game
quadruples the probe bound ($+0.1256$, $p=1.4\times10^{-4}$), and a
diagnostic decomposes the damage into a deployment-blindness channel
(the stored profile is also the deployment's opponent model, and a
masked model assigns zero likelihood to observed reality) worth about a
quarter, and intrinsic equilibrium distortion worth the rest. Pruning
one's own play is defense; pruning the world model is blindness. Every
number is regenerated by a named script or artifact.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Tabular solving of a large imperfect-information game begins with a
decomposition. The game is cut into subgames along a public boundary,
each subgame is solved against the stored values of its successors, and
the deployed profile is the union of the per-subgame policies. The
soundness of this bootstrap is classical \cite{burch2014}, but it comes
with a condition that is easy to state and expensive to ignore: the
values a parent reads must be well-defined quantities, and in a zero-sum
game only the subgame \emph{value} is unique, while its decomposition
over private types is not. Any solver that selects arbitrarily among
equilibria hands each parent a slightly different game, and the
selection noise composes up the bootstrap.

This paper is an experimental study of that selection problem in
heads-up Red/Black, a two-player zero-sum card game of hidden
information that our companion series counts, abstracts, and solves
exactly \cite{rb1,rb2,rb3}. The study's instrument is a
belief-carrying local best-response probe that lower-bounds
exploitability in the \emph{real} game rather than in the solved
abstraction, and its subject is a pair of surgeries on the solving
pipeline that sit at the same joint but point in opposite directions.

The first surgery changes \emph{how the solver selects} among the
equilibria of the game it is given. Prior work in this series found that
CFR+ selection freedom, composed through the bootstrap, produces value
tables that violate an exact automorphism of the game by $1.67$ on a
$[-1,+1]$ scale, and that replacing the per-subgame solver with an
annealed quantal-response (QRE) ladder repairs this to $3.5\times10^{-9}$
\cite{rb2}. The repair, however, costs playing strength: the annealed
ladder's smoothed policies measurably lose ground on the probe relative
to the sharp CFR+ profile they were built to replace. The resolution
this paper reports is a coupling: keep the QRE ladder's values, frozen
byte-identically, and re-solve every subgame's \emph{policy} with CFR+
against those fixed values. The coupled profile passes every internal
acceptance check and is the first profile in the program's history whose
belief-carrying probe lower bound sits at zero.

The second surgery changes \emph{what game the solver is given}. The
solved abstraction provably rewards an action that is dominated in the
real game \cite{rb2}, and play-time guards that prune it from the
deployed policy cut the probe's edge to a third (measured,
$p=0.014$). The natural next step, removing the action from the solved
game itself so that the solve never learns it, fails badly: the masked
solve's probe bound is four times the incumbent's
($p=1.4\times10^{-4}$). A diagnostic arm separates the failure into two
mechanisms with measured weights, and the asymmetry between the two
surgeries is the paper's organizing lesson. Selection can be changed
freely provided the values are pinned; the solved game cannot be pruned,
because the stored profile is simultaneously the deployment's model of
its opponent, and a model that assigns probability zero to actions real
opponents play goes blind at exactly the moments it is being exploited.

Contributions:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{A measured decomposition of solver error.} Value
        correctness under composition and policy strength at deployment
        are supplied by different selection principles: the QRE ladder's
        uniqueness fixes the former and its smoothing damages the
        latter, each with its own instrument reading
        (Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}).
  \item \textbf{A coupling architecture.} Per-subgame CFR+ re-solves
        against frozen QRE values, with byte-identity of the value store
        as an explicit invariant and the policy store as the only
        replaced artifact (Section~\ref{sec:coupling}).
  \item \textbf{The probe-zero result.} The coupled profile is the first
        the belief-carrying probe cannot beat. At $n=10{,}000$ paired
        games the probe loses to it, $\epsG \ge -0.0248$
        $[-0.0444,-0.0052]$, and the comparison against the incumbent
        resolves at $p=3.0\times10^{-5}$
        (Section~\ref{sec:verdict}).
  \item \textbf{A pruning pathology with a mechanism decomposition.}
        Masking a dominated action in the solved game quadruples the
        probe bound; a support-filter diagnostic attributes about a
        quarter of the regression to deployment blindness and the
        remainder to intrinsic equilibrium distortion
        (Section~\ref{sec:pruning}).
  \item \textbf{Equivariance as a meter.} Exact game automorphisms,
        measured and never enforced, are the acceptance instrument
        throughout; the one recorded enforcement blinded the only meter
        that could see the defect it hid
        (Sections~\ref{sec:decomposition} and~\ref{sec:coupling}).
\end{itemize}

Throughout, the claims are disciplined by a pre-registered distinction:
a screen at $3{,}200$ games per arm can support non-inferiority only,
and any improvement claim requires a $10{,}000$-games-per-arm
extension. Both were run, in that order, and the screen's favorable but
unresolved reading ($p=0.154$) was published as non-inferiority until
the extension resolved it (Section~\ref{sec:verdict}).

\section{The game and its abstraction}\label{sec:game}

\subsection{Rules sketch}

Red/Black is played here heads-up with one shared $52$-card deck of
exactly $26$ red and $26$ black cards; only colour matters. Each player
is dealt five cards, and hands only ever shrink. A round has four
phases. In \emph{reorder}, each player privately orders their own hand;
index $0$ is the top. In \emph{bidding}, the starting player bids a pair
(count, colour), asserting that at least that many cards of that colour
are in play across both hands; play alternates, and each turn the active
player either raises (same colour, strictly higher count) or challenges.
A challenge triggers \emph{procurement}: the bidder repeatedly flips the
current top card of either player's deck, and must reveal count cards of
the bid colour before revealing a single off-colour card. The first
off-colour flip loses the contest for the bidder; reaching the count
loses it for the challenger. In \emph{discard}, the loser secretly
removes one card from their hand; a player whose hand reaches zero is
eliminated, and the last player standing wins. Flips are public during
the round they occur in and are hidden again at the next reorder;
discards are anonymous, so an observer can never tell whether a
previously seen card left the hand. The full rules, precise enough to
implement an engine from, are in the repository's \code{RULES.md} and in
\cite{rb1}.

\subsection{The real game and the abstraction}

Write $\game$ for heads-up Red/Black. Exact enumeration \cite{rb1}
(script \code{real\_game\_size.py}) counts $5.3\times10^{34}$ public
decision histories and $2.448\times10^{33}$ perfect-recall information
sets, so no tabular method touches $\game$ directly. The same paper
proves that at every round boundary the reachable public posterior over
the two seats' private types factorizes as a strategy-independent class
prior times two per-seat tilt vectors, where a \emph{class} is the
public state at a procurement resolution (hand sizes, revealed-colour
bounds, and the round's public outcome data).

\begin{definition}[The abstraction $\gp$ \cite{rb1,rb2}]\label{def:gp}
$\gp$ is the game identical to $\game$ within each round, except that at
every round boundary the joint law over type pairs is reset to the class
prior; equivalently, the tilts of the factorization are replaced by $1$.
\end{definition}

Because every round removes exactly one card, the classes form a layered
directed acyclic graph indexed by $T'$, the total number of cards in
play, running from $T'=10$ at the root down to $T'=2$. The solved corpus
has $5{,}721$ classes. A solve proceeds by backward induction over
layers: each class is solved as a two-player zero-sum subgame whose
leaves are priced from the stored values of its successor classes (the
\emph{bootstrap}). For each class the store holds the game value, the
per-type-pair value decomposition $\vtypes$ (the cells parents actually
read), and the behavioural policy tables.

\begin{definition}[Round-local and real-game exploitability]
\label{def:eps}
$\epsround$ of a class solve is the exact best-response gap of its final
policy \emph{within that class's subgame}, leaves fixed at the stored
successor values. $\epsG$ of a deployed profile is its exploitability in
the real game $\game$. All payoffs are in $[-1,+1]$, so both quantities
live on a range of $2.0$.
\end{definition}

$\epsround$ is what a solver can drive to tolerance; $\epsG$ is what an
opponent experiences. The distance between them is the abstraction's
cost plus everything the deployment adds or repairs, and the entire
methodological content of this paper is that only instruments aimed at
$\epsG$ are allowed to decide anything.

\section{The instrument}\label{sec:instrument}

\subsection{The belief-carrying probe}

The probe (\code{scripts/probe\_resolver.py}, \cite{rb3}) plays a local
best responder (LBR \cite{lisy2017}) against the deployed profile under
\emph{full belief carrying}: the probe maintains the exact public
posterior induced by the profile's own tables, rather than the
abstraction's reset prior, and best-responds against the profile's
actual continuation. Design elements that the numbers below depend on:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \emph{Shadow replay.} A shadow copy of the deployed
        resolver runs beside the probed one and their action
        distributions are asserted identical at read-back, so the probe
        provably measures the profile that is actually deployed rather
        than a reconstruction of it.
  \item \emph{Paired seats and matched seeds.} Every deal is played
        twice with seats mirrored, under identical decision seeds; arms
        being compared use the same seed set (seeds \code{pvr}
        $0$--$1599$, $1{,}600$ pairs, $n=3{,}200$ games per arm at
        screen size).
  \item \emph{Wilson confidence intervals} on the probe's win rate
        $\mathrm{wr}$, and the conversion
        $\epsG \ge 2\,(\mathrm{wr}-\tfrac12)$, so every reading is a
        \emph{lower bound} on real-game exploitability. The instrument
        cannot certify an upper bound (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
\end{itemize}

Belief carrying is what gives the probe teeth: against the incumbent
profile's raw stored tables the bound is $0.598$ per game, and full
carrying by the deployed resolver cuts it to $0.078$ \cite{rb3}, so the
residual the probe measures is the part of the abstraction's cost that
deployment does not repair.

\subsection{Screens and extensions}\label{sec:screens}

At $n=3{,}200$ the probe resolves differences of roughly $0.035$, and
the incumbent's entire post-guards residual is $+0.031$. A screen at
this size can therefore support \emph{non-inferiority} only. The
program's pre-registered rule: every candidate is screened at the
benchmark's exact seeds and size; only candidates whose point estimate
moves the right way buy a $10{,}000$-to-$20{,}000$-games-per-arm
extension; and because screening several candidates and extending the
best is a selection procedure, the extension, not the screen, is the
honest test of an improvement claim. No sentence below claims more than
its instrument resolves.

\section{The measured decomposition}\label{sec:decomposition}

This section assembles the three measurements that motivate the
coupling: CFR+ selection freedom corrupts composed values; an annealed
QRE ladder repairs the values; and the same ladder's policies give up
playing strength. The section's conclusion is one sentence, and it is
load-bearing: nothing forces one method to produce both products.

\subsection{CFR+ selection freedom, seen by an automorphism}
\label{sec:cfrfree}

Red/Black's deck has exactly $26$ red and $26$ black cards and no other
card attribute exists, so swapping the colours is an automorphism of
$\game$ and of $\gp$ (the class prior is colour-symmetric). The value of
a class must equal the value of its colour-swapped image with types
remapped, exactly, for any equilibrium. The solver never enforces this,
which is what makes it a meter.

Measured on the CFR+ solve of record, solve-2: of $2{,}971$ colour
orbits, $2{,}206$ violate, with worst $\vtypes$ cell error $1.67$ on
the $[-1,+1]$ scale, while the same classes report $\epsround$ between
$5\times10^{-5}$ and $10^{-4}$ (script \path{check_symmetry.py},
artifact \path{symmetry_full.json}). The defect is invisible to every
convergence meter by construction, because each class solve is
internally consistent with the corrupted leaves it was handed. A
discriminating experiment (script \path{symmetry_discriminate.py},
artifact \path{symmetry_discriminate.json}) exonerates the game logic
entirely: re-solving violating orbits with leaves forced symmetric
lands every twin equivariant to machine precision, while raw store
leaves differing by at most $10^{-4}$ produce cell jumps of
$0.14$--$0.45$. The mechanism is equilibrium-selection freedom. The
subgame value is unique but $\vtypes$ is not; CFR+ selects arbitrarily
among equilibrium decompositions, self-swap classes seed
$\sim4\times10^{-3}$ of cell asymmetry on perfectly symmetric inputs,
an ill-conditioned cell map amplifies input differences roughly
$1000\times$, and parents inherit genuinely different games \cite{rb2}.

\begin{remark}[An enforced invariant is a blinded meter, 2026-08]
\label{rem:enforce}
An earlier solve in this series enforced the seat-mirror symmetry by
construction, after which the mirror gap read zero because it was forced
to. The colour swap, which nothing enforced, later read violations of
the same $\sim0.11$ magnitude on the enforced solve: the defect had been
hidden, not fixed \cite{rb2}. Every symmetry in this paper is measured
and never imposed, and the coupling's acceptance checks
(Section~\ref{sec:coupling}) inherit that rule unchanged.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The annealed QRE ladder}\label{sec:qre}

The repair is to make the per-class selection unique. The QRE ladder
(driver \path{qre_solve.py}, kernel mode \code{QRE}) replaces CFR+
within each class by magnetic mirror descent \cite{sokota2022} on an
entropy-regularized game: per information-set row,
\[
  \ell \;\leftarrow\; \frac{\ell + \eta\, q / c}{1 + \eta\tau},
\]
with $q$ the counterfactual action value normalized by counterfactual
reach $c$, step size $\eta=4$, and the temperature $\tau$ annealed down
a fixed geometric ladder from $0.3$ to $\tau_f = 3\times10^{-3}$,
taking the last iterate. For every
$\tau>0$ the regularized game has a unique equilibrium, continuous in
its leaf inputs, so the composition problem of
Section~\ref{sec:cfrfree} is solved by construction rather than by
tolerance. Measured on the full re-solve (solve-4): colour equivariance
holds on all $2{,}971$ orbits with worst residual $3.5\times10^{-9}$
per cell, against the CFR+ baseline's $1.67$
(\code{check\_symmetry.py}; \cite{rb2}).

Cost matters because the ladder must be re-runnable. A value-based
stopping rule (stop a rung when the class value stops moving, rather
than on an iteration budget) cuts the ladder roughly $30\times$, from
$40{,}406$ iterations per class to about $1{,}300$, and was validated on
the full $360$-orbit policy-equivariance sweep rather than a pilot: the
divergent-twin tail shrinks from $22$ to $6$ orbits with overall
$\varepsilon$ quality unchanged (worst-twin median $0.0030$ before and
after; artifact \code{deps\_value\_stoprule\_full.json}). The fast
ladder is what makes every experiment in
Sections~\ref{sec:coupling} and~\ref{sec:pruning} affordable.

\subsection{What the ladder costs: smoothed policies are weak}
\label{sec:qrecost}

The QRE profile's values are right and its play is not. In the arena the
annealed solve ties the CFR+ incumbent ($0.4970$ $[0.479, 0.515]$,
$p=0.76$, $n=3{,}000$; \code{docs/solve-3-4-qre.md}), which by
Section~\ref{sec:arena} decides nothing. The probe decides: with
play-time guards on both profiles, the belief-carrying bound against
the QRE profile is $+0.076$ $[+0.014,+0.137]$ at $n=1{,}000$
(\code{s5\_probe\_solve4\_guards.json}) against the CFR+ incumbent's
$+0.031$ $[-0.004,+0.065]$ at $n=3{,}200$
(\code{s5\_probe\_solve2\_guards.json} and \code{\_ext.json}). The
ladder's full-support, temperature-smoothed policies concede ground
that the sharp CFR+ policies do not, at the same time as its values
pass an exactness test the CFR+ values fail by nine orders of
magnitude.

The decomposition is now measured on both sides. Value correctness
under composition needs uniqueness and equivariance; deployment
strength needs sharp support. One selection principle supplies each,
and nothing forces one method to produce both.

\section{The coupling}\label{sec:coupling}

\subsection{Architecture}

The coupled solve (store \code{solve-6-cfrpolicy}, driver
\code{scripts/policy\_pass.py}) re-solves every one of the $5{,}721$
classes with CFR+ at tolerance $10^{-5}$ (achieved median
$\approx10^{-5}$) against leaf prices taken from the QRE ladder's value
store and held \emph{fixed}: the CFR+ pass reads solve-4's $\vtypes$
cells and never writes values back. The deployed value artifact is
solve-4's \code{values.pkl.gz}, byte-identical (sha256-verified), plus
new policy tables. Two design decisions carry the result.

\emph{Frozen values are load-bearing.} CFR+ within a class converges to
its own arbitrary equilibrium decomposition, and letting its values
flow back into the store would re-import the exact selection noise of
Section~\ref{sec:cfrfree}. The size of the freedom being discarded was
measured. Over the full 5{,}721-class pass, CFR+'s own $\vtypes$ cells
drift from the held QRE prices by as much as $1.4244$; on a stratified
90-class re-solve sample the per-class maximum cell drift has median
$0.0293$, 95th percentile $0.5822$, and maximum $0.7324$, while the
entry-weighted class values of the same solves agree with the held
prices to a median of $1.0\times10^{-4}$ and never differ by more than
$1.1\times10^{-3}$ (\code{coupling\_value\_drift.py}, artifact
\code{coupling\_value\_drift.json}). Four orders of magnitude separate
the cell drift from the value drift: both solvers agree about what the
class is worth and disagree about how to decompose that worth across
type pairs, which is $\vtypes$ non-uniqueness doing exactly what
Section~\ref{sec:cfrfree} says it does.

\emph{One price, every consumer.} Internal consistency is a stated
invariant: the value store every parent bootstraps from, the prices the
deployed resolver's leaf evaluations use, and the cells the analysis
tools display are one artifact, and the sha256 identity of
\code{values.pkl.gz} with its solve-4 source is checked as an
acceptance condition, so no consumer can drift onto a second price.

\subsection{Acceptance checks}

The feared cost of the coupling was the return of per-class selection
freedom in the \emph{policies}. The policy-equivariance sweep (script
\path{check_policy_symmetry.py}, artifact
\path{policy_symmetry_solve-6-cfrpolicy.json}) measures the policy
distance $\deps$ across all $360$ audited colour orbits: $360/360$
records, median $1.2\times10^{-9}$, maximum $0.000$. The cost never
materialized, and the reason is worth recording: deterministic CFR+ is
an equivariant map, so over colour-equivariant inputs (the frozen QRE
prices) it returns colour-equivariant policies. The selection noise of
Section~\ref{sec:cfrfree} was never CFR+ acting alone; it was CFR+
composing its own corrupted inputs up the bootstrap, and pinning the
inputs removes the seed.

The remaining checks are shaped by the incumbent. Tripwire telemetry
(script \path{tripwires.py}, artifact
\path{tripwires_solve-6-cfrpolicy.json}) reads incumbent-shaped: the
coupled profile is unmasked and plays the $\gp$-equilibrium rate of the
real-game-dominated action natively, and play-time guards keep handling
it at deployment, a division of labor that Section~\ref{sec:pruning}
justifies by measurement. Compute for the full pass: $67{,}982$ seconds
of wall clock, about $19$ hours including a five-hour suspension, on
one 10-core Apple M-series machine with 32~GB of memory
(\code{policy\_pass.py} run record in
\code{simulator/logs/solve-5-journal.md}).

Section~\ref{sec:screens} predicts for a true difference of this
magnitude. The extension resolves it. Pooled at $10{,}000$ games per
arm the difference is $-0.0590$, $z=-4.17$, $p=3.0\times10^{-5}$, and
the claim of record moves from non-inferiority to improvement.

The extension alone carries that claim, which matters because the
screen selected this candidate and screening several candidates then
extending the best is a selection procedure. On the $6{,}800$ fresh
games that took no part in the selection, the difference is $-0.0700$,
$z=-4.08$, $p=4.5\times10^{-5}$, slightly larger than the pooled
figure. Nothing here depends on reusing the screen.

The sign carries the more interesting reading. The probe does not
merely fail to beat the coupled profile, it loses to it, $0.4876$
against a fair $0.5$ ($p=0.014$ pooled, $p=0.005$ on the fresh games).
A negative lower bound is vacuous, since $\epsG \ge 0$ holds by
definition, so the honest statement of the absolute result is that the
belief-carrying probe yields no positive lower bound on this profile's
exploitability at $n=10{,}000$. That is a fact about the instrument
rather than a proof of near-optimality, and it is the sense in which
this program now lacks a probe strong enough to price its own best
profile. The instrument is otherwise healthy: the same probe at the
same seeds with the same guards reads $+0.0342$ with a confidence
interval clear of zero against the incumbent, which is what makes the
comparative claim well posed even though the absolute one is not.

Because the two arms were measured on different machines, both stores
were fingerprinted file by file on each before pooling. The coupled
store is bit-identical across machines, and the incumbent's differs
only in a Finder metadata file and in ablation directories no probe
reads. \code{pool\_probe.py} compares the runs' configuration keys but
not the stores' contents, and the \code{store} field it carries is a
filesystem path, so this check is external to the tooling by
construction.

The arena control is consistent with non-regression and decides nothing
further: against the incumbent, $0.5096$ $[0.496,0.523]$, $p=0.18$
guards-off and $0.5048$ $[0.491,0.519]$, $p=0.51$ guards-on, at
$n=5{,}000$ per mode (artifact
\code{eval\_solve-6-cfrpolicy\_vs\_solve2.json}). Per
Section~\ref{sec:arena} the arena's role here is only to rule out gross
breakage, and it does.

\section{The contrast arm: pruning the solved game}\label{sec:pruning}

The coupling changed how the solver selects. The contrast arm changed
what game it solves, and its failure is what locates the boundary
between the two kinds of surgery.

\subsection{Background: a dominated action the abstraction rewards}

The solved abstraction rewards an action that the real game punishes:
at some procurement information sets, flipping one's own known
off-colour card (a \emph{suicide flip}, guaranteed to lose the contest)
is simultaneously optimal in $\gp$ and dominated in $\game$, because
the belief reset launders the self-revelation the flip would cost in
the real game \cite{rb2}. The $\gp$ equilibrium plays it with
probability up to $0.97$ on-path. Two independent solvers corroborate
the artifact: the CFR+ era measured $p=0.97$ with $0.0499$ of the
affected class's value attached, and the QRE ladder, a different engine
and update rule, returns $p=0.96$ and $0.0497$
(\code{tests/test\_solver\_dominance.py};
\code{simulator/logs/solve-5-journal.md}).

Play-time guards, which prune the action from the deployed policy's own
play and touch nothing else, are measured to help: on the incumbent the
probe bound falls from $+0.0919$ $[+0.0573,+0.1262]$ guards-off to
$+0.0306$ $[-0.0040,+0.0652]$ guards-on, $z=2.45$, $p=0.014$ at
$n=3{,}200$ per arm (artifacts \code{s5\_probe\_solve2\_base.json},
\code{s5\_probe\_solve2\_guards.json}, with \code{\_ext} extensions).
Guards cut the probe's edge to a third. The tempting inference is that
removing the action from the solved game itself, so no store ever
learns it, should do at least as well.

\subsection{The masked solve}

The masked solve (store \code{solve-5-pruned}) runs the identical fast
QRE ladder with the suicide flip made illegal inside every class
subgame. Provenance is read back from the finished policies rather than
from the configuration that requested them: $2{,}209/2{,}209$ canonical
class-solves masked, $14{,}777{,}285$ policy rows locked, worst
residual mass on any locked row exactly $0$ (append-only record
\code{solve-meta.jsonl}; colour-swap and $\deps$ checks in
\code{symmetry\_solve-5-pruned.json} and
\code{policy\_symmetry\_solve-5-pruned.json}). The deployment masks its
resolves the same way, so the deployed strategy space is the solved
one. The verdict, at the benchmark's seeds and size:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
arm & probe wr & $\epsG$ lower bound \\
\midrule
solve-5-pruned (masked, guards on) & $0.5628$ &
  $+0.1256$ $[+0.0911,+0.1598]$ \\
solve-2, guards on (benchmark) & $0.5153$ & $+0.0306$
  $[-0.0040,+0.0652]$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Difference $+0.095$, $z=3.81$, $p=1.4\times10^{-4}$; the intervals do
not overlap (artifact \path{pool_solve-5-pruned_vs_solve2.json}).
Baking into the solve the same removal that helped at play time
\emph{quadrupled} the probe's bound.

\subsection{The diagnostic, and the two mechanisms}

The first suspect surfaced in telemetry: $806$ of $3{,}200$ probe games
against the masked profile broke the deployed resolver's opponent
filter, against $0$ of $3{,}200$ in the benchmark. A \emph{break} is
the filter observing an action to which its model assigns zero
likelihood, after which that game softly degrades to static tables. The
masked store makes this structural. The stored profile is also the
deployment's opponent model; the masked model says the suicide flip has
probability zero; the probe, an external best responder correctly not
subject to the mask, plays it when the reset artifact makes it
attractive; and the filter then cannot explain observed reality.

The diagnostic arm re-probes the \emph{same} masked store with the
filter's opponent floor extended to structural zeros
(\code{--opp-support-filter}), same seeds, same size. Breaks fall from
$806/3{,}200$ to $0/3{,}200$, and the bound falls from $+0.1256$ to
$+0.0931$ $[+0.0585,+0.1275]$, which is still decisively worse than the
benchmark ($z=2.50$, $p=0.012$; artifact
\path{pool_solve-5-pruned-osf_vs_solve2.json}). The decomposition:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \emph{Deployment blindness, about a quarter of the regression.}
        Eliminating the break channel recovers $0.126 \to 0.093$. The
        mechanism is general: whenever the stored profile doubles as
        the deployment's opponent model, pruning the store creates a
        model that zero-likelihoods actions real opponents play, and
        inference dies exactly when it is needed.
  \item \emph{Intrinsic equilibrium distortion, the remaining three
        quarters.} The masked $\gp$ equilibrium prices every
        surrounding decision against an action set no real opponent is
        restricted to. The removed action is genuinely $\gp$-optimal at
        $p=0.96$--$0.97$, and masking it surrenders about $0.05$ of the
        affected class's value; the whole strategy shifts around that
        surrender, and the shifted strategy is what the real game
        exploits.
\end{itemize}

One caveat is carried rather than hidden: the support filter also
coarsens opponent inference generally, so the diagnostic is not a
surgical break-only ablation; but with breaks at exactly zero and the
bound within noise of the verdict's, no reading of it rescues the
mask. The closing statement of the contrast arm, earned by three
measurements (guards help at $p=0.014$; the mask hurts at
$p=1.4\times10^{-4}$; the diagnostic splits the damage): pruning your
own play is defense, and pruning your model of the world is blindness.
A dominated action leaves the deployed policy at play time, or it does
not leave at all.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\emph{Decomposition and re-solving.} Subgame decomposition with stored
summaries is due to Burch et al.\ \cite{burch2014} (CFR-D), whose
soundness argument carries an equilibrium-selection invariance
condition: the stored quantities must mean the same thing to every
subgame that reads them. The QRE ladder of Section~\ref{sec:qre} can be
read as a constructive way to satisfy the uniqueness that decomposition
wants, and the coupling keeps that construction while discarding the
ladder's policies.

\emph{Solver-dependent selection.} The nearest neighbor to
Section~\ref{sec:cfrfree} is the recent characterization of
solver-dependent selection on zero-sum Nash polytopes
\cite{nashselect}, which studies how different algorithms select
different points of the equilibrium set. Our contribution is
complementary and empirical: a measured account of what that freedom
costs when selections \emph{compose} through a value bootstrap
($1.67$ per cell), and a deployed architecture that pins the selection
where composition needs it and frees it where deployment does.

\emph{MMD and QRE.} Sokota et al.\ \cite{sokota2022} introduce the
magnetic mirror descent update we use and anneal it toward a Nash
\emph{policy}. Our reading of the same ladder inside a decomposition is
different: the ladder's deliverable is the \emph{values}, whose
uniqueness and equivariance are what composition consumes, and we
measure that its annealed policy should be discarded
(Section~\ref{sec:qrecost}).

\emph{Learned value functions.} DeepStack \cite{deepstack}, value
functions for depth-limited solving \cite{kovarik2019}, ReBeL
\cite{rebel}, and Student of Games \cite{sog} price subgame leaves with
\emph{learned approximate} value functions for the sake of scale. The
present system is the small-scale exact counterpart: both selection
principles are exact solvers, chosen from a measured error
decomposition, with the values frozen byte-identically rather than
approximated. What transfers in either direction is the coupling
question itself, since any decomposed agent must decide which product
each component is trusted for.

\emph{Abstraction and pruning pathologies.} Waugh et al.\
\cite{waugh2009} showed that refining an abstraction can hurt the
resulting equilibrium. Section~\ref{sec:pruning} is a \emph{pruning}
pathology with a mechanism decomposition: restricting the solved game's
action set with a real-game-motivated, provably dominated removal
quadrupled measured real-game exploitability, and a quarter of the
damage flows through a channel we have not found documented, the
deployment's use of its own store as its opponent model. Regret-based
pruning \cite{brownsandholm2015} sharpens the distinction from the
other side: it prunes \emph{computation} while provably preserving the
solution, whereas the mask prunes the \emph{game}, and the two must not
be conflated under the one word.

We characterize the system's novelty as a \emph{measured coupling}: the
components are standard, and the contribution is the error
decomposition that says which component supplies which product, with an
instrument reading behind each arrow.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\emph{One game.} Every measurement is from heads-up Red/Black. The
mechanisms are stated generally (selection noise composing through a
bootstrap; a store doubling as an opponent model), but their weights
($1.67$; a quarter versus three quarters) are this game's.

\emph{A lower-bound instrument.} The probe certifies $\epsG$ from below
only. The companion certificate program pursued an upper bound and
reports its obstruction honestly \cite{rb3}: leaf-price dishonorability
of $0.11$--$0.26$ per re-solve is intrinsic to depth-limited resolving
from any stored belief-reset surface, and no upper bound below the
trivial $2.0$ is claimed. Probe-zero therefore means the instrument
cannot beat the profile, and does not mean the profile is unbeatable.
The coupled profile has now passed the point where this instrument
says anything absolute about it at all: the bound it returns is
negative, hence vacuous, and only the comparison against a profile the
probe can still beat remains informative. Pricing the coupled profile
absolutely requires a stronger probe, and building one is open.

\emph{Temperature bias in the values.} The deployed values are the QRE
ladder's at $\tau_f=3\times10^{-3}$, and carry its bias: the root value
of $\gp$ differs across the CFR+ and QRE solvers at the third decimal
($+0.0245$ versus $+0.0190$; \cite{rb2},
\code{docs/solve-3-4-qre.md}). Composition consumes the values'
uniqueness, not their unbiasedness, but any consumer quoting a cell as
ground truth inherits the $\tau_f$ floor.

\emph{Provenance.} This is a single-author study of a bespoke domain.
The mitigations are structural: every number in this paper names the
script or artifact that regenerates it, the game rules are specified to
engine-implementable precision \cite{rb1}, and the probe's shadow-replay
and read-back assertions are designed so that the measured object is
provably the deployed one.
